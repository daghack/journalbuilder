The bells of Saint Ysolt I have rung eleven years, and her graves I have dug for nine, and I set down here what the vicar will not hear. On the eve of Candlemas the fen came up over the lychgate and the water stood in the nave to the knees of the saints. When it went down she was there before the altar, the one they drowned, with her hair standing up about her as if the water had not let her go. She did not speak. She lifted her arms and the thirteen candles lit of themselves. Since that night the bells ring when no hand is on the ropes. I count them. Thirteen, and then three together, and then three more, as if a name were being spelt and I too slow to read it. The rooks will not settle on the tower. The yew by the north wall has put out red berries in the dead of winter. I have opened her grave by lantern light and it is empty, and the earth in it is wet, though no rain has fallen this month. She walks at vespers. I have seen her pass between the pillars of the nave and the stones did not ring under her feet. She does not look at me. She looks at the rose window, where the glass shows a maze and not the lamb, and I swear it showed the lamb when I was a boy. Whoever finds this book, do not ring the bells to call her back. Do not answer them when they ring. Do not stand at the rail of the churchyard after dark, for the fence is old and she wrote on it with her finger, and the iron has kept the words. I have copied what I could. The rest I have hidden the way she taught me, in columns and in circles and in the counting of stars, so that none who comes after can read it by accident. Pray for the sexton of Saint Ysolt. He did not pray for himself.
